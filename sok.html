Better substitutions

When the product isn’t on the shelf

Client: S Group / S-kaupat · Timeframe: May–June 2026 · Type: Discovery · Status: In progress

S-kaupat is S Group’s online grocery service. Every order carries a quiet risk: by the time the picker reaches the shelf, the product the customer ordered may not be there.

S-kaupat wanted to find new ways to substitute better, both when a product is actually out of stock and when the data already predicts it won’t be available on the delivery day.

I led the discovery end to end. I planned and facilitated all three workshops, analysed the availability data, synthesised the findings into root causes and a prioritised roadmap, and designed the first concepts.

Project approach

A three-workshop discovery that moved from data, through employees’ experience, to the end customer. I designed the process and the workshop materials, facilitated every session and turned the outputs into the final synthesis.

Three root causes

The availability and substitution problem wasn’t one broken thing. It was three that made each other worse.

1.
Availability data can’t be fully trusted. Stock levels, shelving rhythm, assortment changes and forecasts don’t always match reality at picking time. The result is unnecessary shortages and substitutions, and customers who lose trust when the delivery doesn’t match the basket.
2.
Communication doesn’t make uncertainty manageable. Information is spread across channels, and there’s no single place to see what happened to an order. When customers don’t understand what’s going on, uncertainty turns into distrust.
3.
Decision support is too weak for both customer and picker. Customers can’t set their own substitution rules, so pickers make judgement calls without personalised guidance. Substitution quality varies and picking slows down.

“The customer is a bystander in their own order.”

Two hypotheses that guided the work

The goal isn’t to make every availability problem disappear. It’s to make those problems understandable and manageable for everyone involved.

Picker

Hypothesis

When availability data is up to date and substitution logic is clear, pickers can decide quickly, without guesswork or interruptions.

Customer

Hypothesis

When the customer’s choices, real-time information and automated decisions come together in clear communication, the customer always knows what to expect.

From insight to priorities

These were followed by picker decision support, probability-based availability messaging, and a dynamic interface that steers customers to better delivery slots.

Priorities
1.
Fix the availability data. Without reliable data, better communication or picker support can’t deliver real change.
2.
Real-time communication with the customer. Approve, edit and give feedback on substitutions during and after picking. The customer takes part in the order instead of being surprised by it.
3.
Customer-defined substitution rules. Substitute, don’t substitute, or only a specific product, plus price tolerance and allergy and dislike profiles. Substitution becomes a designed part of the service rather than something that feels like an error.

New and improved picking data

As part of a major initiative focused on improving in-store picking operations, we concepted a completely new way of presenting pickers’ daily and weekly performance metrics. The aim was to make the numbers clear and directly supportive of day-to-day execution on the shop floor.

The starting point was that pickers could see weekly picking data, but the numbers and the logic behind them were not understandable or actionable in day-to-day work. During H1/2025, we gathered feedback on the existing views and used that input to drive the key design decisions.

We then launched new daily and weekly data views in beta across 10 stores, and collected structured feedback via an in-device survey on the Keräytin.

The outcome has been clearly positive: the new views are perceived as a significant improvement compared to the previous ones, and they support daily performance management more effectively.

Boost for the picking efficiency

Continuing with the Similar to Same theme, there was also a need to coach pickers to do their job at the right pace. For that we concepted and tested new ways of presenting data after each picking round.

Tested through interviews with the store pickers the round ending data view evolved to its current state. Presented information was found relative and quick to comprehend.
